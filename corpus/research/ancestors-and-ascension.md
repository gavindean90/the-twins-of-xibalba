# The Ancestors and the Ascension

## Finding and confidence

The parenthetical fathers in Brasseur and Guthrie are **editorial subject identifications**, not an explicit fathers noun in the manuscript’s ascent clause. The manuscript supports a sun-and-moon ascent by a plural subject. Its narrative continuity favors the victorious younger twins, but the short clause alone neither names that pair nor allocates Sun and Moon individually. The father/uncle restoration passage immediately before it remains difficult. This is a qualified philological conclusion, not a majority vote or a justification retrofitted to the poem.

## Narrative sequence — source evidence

After displaying their sacrificial and restorative powers, the younger twins kill the leading lords and do not restore them. The followers flee and submit. The twins disclose their identities and their connection to the earlier victims, then restrict Xibalba’s future claims. The passage describes diminished authority and changed offerings; it does not demonstrate abolition of all death. M 31r–31v, sequences 71–72; S pp. 75–77; B II.xiii–xiv; `pv-victory`.

The narrative then returns to Xmucane and the planted canes, which wither and revive, producing grief, incense offerings and commemoration. After this household scene it turns to the ancestors. Attempts concerning the father’s face and naming do not yield complete restoration; the descendants promise invocation and remembrance and identify themselves as avengers of the earlier suffering. Then comes ascent, one becoming Sun and another Moon, followed by the four hundred youths becoming stars/stellar companions. M 32r–32v, sequences 73–74; S pp. 78–79; B pp. 190–193; G May p. 111; `pv-father-reunion` and `pv-ascension`.

## Why the parenthesis is not decisive

B does more than clarify an isolated ambiguous pronoun. He prepares the celestial reading earlier by adding a heavenly location to the promise of ancestral precedence. G repeats that network of explanatory choices, not just the single parenthesis. The distinctive shared additions support dependence at these loci. The existing qualified edition-level relationship is retained; this limited comparison does not prove every paragraph was translated exclusively from French.

The manuscript’s consecutive ascent clauses do not themselves announce a switch from the younger actors to their fathers. The surrounding distinction between consoling descendants and ancestors left at the named place weighs against inserting such a switch without further argument. Nevertheless, absence of an explicit noun is not grammatical proof that only one antecedent is possible. A specialist must analyze discourse reference across the whole paragraph, including the singular/plural shifts in the preceding ancestral scene.

## Restoration, identity and modern scholarship

B p. 191 explicitly singles out Vukub-Hunahpu for the ancestral honors, and G May p. 111 §15 repeats that identification. S p. 78 instead has Hun-ahpu in its difficult encounter sentence. The manuscript left column near the bottom of 32r visibly includes the Vucub Hunahpu name, but the whole construction has not been transcribed or grammatically resolved here. This is a reason to preserve the uncle’s distinct role rather than automatically describe every action as restoration of One Hunahpu.

B frames the preceding action as funeral honors, an image and possibly a name sought on a monument; his notes offer reconstructions. S’s wording about finding the father, face and name is awkward. Neither should be converted into a smooth account of complete resurrection. The source’s memorial promise is affirmative even where physical restoration is limited.

Christenson’s electronic edition, pp. 176–177 (narrative lines 4773–4821), reads attempted facial restoration, leaves the father’s heart at the ballcourt and continues with the younger twins’ ascent. His note 449 connects the promised remembrance with ancestral observance. This is a modern K’iche’-informed interpretation, not an additional manuscript witness. His *Original Text Transcription*, f. 32v, was consulted as a separate editorial comparator, not copied into the diplomatic assets.

No individual name occurs beside the sun/moon assignments in the selected ascent clause. “Hunahpu is Sun; Xbalanque is Moon” therefore needs argument from wider tradition, ordering or scholarship; it is more specific than the immediate clause. Identification of the father with a maize deity, or a classical image with this precise scene, requires separate evidence. The four hundred youths should be acknowledged without assuming that the clause alone identifies a modern constellation name.

## Implications for the poem — editorial possibilities

The current Book VII leaves the father below and sends the twins into the sky. The evidence does not require replacing that broad choice with the fathers’ ascent. It does require recognizing that the poem’s individual celestial assignments, extended departure landscape, heavenly ballplay and seasonal closing imagery are literary elaborations. The father’s extended speeches are not a diplomatic source quotation. Preserve remembrance and limits of restoration rather than making vengeance automatically reverse every earlier death.

For an eleven-book plan, allocate space for the reduction of Xibalba’s authority, the grandmother’s memorial response, the ancestral encounter and ascent as distinct transitions. Decide whether the four hundred youths will appear; omitting them is an adaptation choice that needs acknowledgment.

## Outstanding research

Independently transcribe all of 32r–32v, including the difficult father/uncle naming clause and corrections. Obtain a K’iche’ specialist’s account of plural subject continuity and the first-out/first-invoked promise. Compare Sam Colop and other K’iche’ scholars directly rather than cite them only through Christenson. Establish the place-name’s morphology and the identity of the remaining ancestor without silently reconciling the historical versions. This work has not settled those questions.
