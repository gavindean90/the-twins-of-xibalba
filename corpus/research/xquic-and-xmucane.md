# Xquic and Xmucane

## Narrative sequence — source evidence

Xquic arrives while still pregnant and declares herself Xmucane’s daughter-in-law. The older woman challenges her: her sons have died in Xibalba, while Hunbatz and Hunchouen remain as their descendants. Xquic insists on the relationship and on the continuing life represented by what she carries. The household initially rejects her claim. M 17r–17v, sequences 43–44; S p. 42; B II.iv, pp. 100–103; G March II.iv §§1–7; `pv-xquic-xmucane`.

Xmucane then sends her to fetch a large net of maize from the elder boys’ field. Xquic finds only one maize plant/ear rather than an ordinary harvest sufficient for the demand. She laments, invokes powers associated with provision and takes the maize silk without removing the ear. The net fills. Helpers carry it home. Xmucane first suspects the field has been stripped, then inspects it and finds the plant still there and the trace of the net. That evidence establishes Xquic’s claim sufficiently for acceptance. M 17v–18r, sequences 44–45; S pp. 43–44; B pp. 102–105; G March §§8–13.

The birth follows this household test. Xquic is named as mother, the birth occurs away from the grandmother’s observation and the boys subsequently enter the house. Their mistreatment and rivalry with the older brothers follow. Acceptance of Xquic does not mean the household instantly becomes harmonious. M 18r, sequence 45; S p. 44; B II.v, pp. 106–107; `pv-twins-childhood`.

## Primary evidence and historical comparison

On M 17v the Spanish says animals carry the net. B’s facing K’iche’ at p. 104 retains *chicop*, but its French at p. 105 renders the helpers as “barbares.” In note 2, Brasseur explicitly acknowledges Ximénez’s animal rendering and says he preferred the human one. That print page was visually checked. G March p. 371, §12, turns them into “laboring people.” This is a particularly clear example of interpretive propagation: the later English is not independent evidence that human laborers were in the K’iche’ episode.

S p. 43 also gives animals. Its claim that the plant has no ears sits awkwardly with the subsequent taking of the ear’s silk. B instead describes a single sheaf and later an ear; Sp lines 381–386 reduces the event to gathering maize where none grew. Retain the modest secure statement: the requested quantity is unavailable, the silk is manipulated, the net fills and the field remains intact. Do not use Sp to erase the remaining plant, which is indispensable to the grandmother’s inspection.

The opening household transition calls the woman the mother of Hunbatz and Hunchouen in the manuscript Spanish and early printed witnesses. B p. 101 note 2 suggests grandmother; G adds a parenthetical grandmother explanation. The earlier genealogy makes Xmucane grandmother and names Xbaquiyalo as the elder boys’ deceased mother. This is a textual/kinship-language problem to document, not an invitation to invent a second living mother or silently amend the historical witness.

## Philological questions

The invoked names vary: compare the manuscript’s visible forms with S’s Xtoh/Xcanil/Xcanix and B’s Xtoh/Xganil/Xcacou (French page variants also differ). OCR cannot settle those readings. The identities and calendar associations developed in B’s notes are commentary; they should not become dialogue exposition without separate assessment.

The word translated as net, basket or carrying bag needs material precision. Likewise, the relation between silk, ear, plant and sheaf needs clause-level collation. This dossier records the contrast but does not create a falsely exact modern agricultural reconstruction.

Xquic’s assertion that the ancestors live in what she bears belongs alongside the skull’s discourse of descendants and the ending’s ancestral commemoration. Reading that statement as literal bodily resurrection in Xibalba would conflict with the continuing problem the younger twins encounter at the end.

## Her subsequent role — printed evidence, manuscript collation pending

Xquic remains present beyond the birth. S p. 48 has the younger twins address both grandmother and mother after the older brothers' transformation (local lines 2175–2181). During retrieval of the hidden ball equipment, they first send Xmucane to fetch water, then send their mother after her; both women try to stop the vessel's leak while the rat releases the equipment (pp. 53–54, lines 2325–2361). The farewell explicitly addresses grandmother and mother and leaves planted canes as a sign of the boys' condition (p. 58, lines 2477–2488). These three spans are now checksum-anchored under the existing childhood, ballgame and descent IDs.

These passages show continued household presence and involvement, including being outwitted by her own sons. They do not establish that she directs the descent or participates in every later exploit. The Spanish printed text provides these locators; manuscript-level collation and a complete inventory of subsequent references are still pending. Calling her merely an explanation of conception misses even the explicit later Spanish sequence.

## Interpretation and implications for the poem

The maize test is a test of kinship and provisioning as well as a marvel. Xquic must gain admission to a bereaved household whose existing members have interests and suspicions of their own. Her successful action supplies evidence where her account alone has failed. The older woman’s inspection matters: she does not accept a declaration simply because the narrator knows it is true.

For an expanded narrative, preserve Xquic’s claim, the seemingly impossible demand, her invocation and action, then the inspection and recognition. The helper discrepancy offers a clear research decision: the animal reading is supported by Ximénez’s Spanish and acknowledged even by Brasseur, while his human substitution needs labeling as interpretation. Her later prominence diminishes as the twins’ exploits take over; this does not authorize an invented death or disappearance scene. A full inventory of later mother references remains outstanding.

## Outstanding research

Transcribe the maize invocation and carrying clause. Review kinship terms with a K’iche’ specialist, particularly “mother,” “grandmother” and daughter-in-law. Extend the three printed anchors above into a complete manuscript-backed inventory of later mother references, including the field episode, so that her subsequent role is neither exaggerated nor erased. No comprehensive negative claim about her final appearance is made here.
