Book 4 of VII

The Borrowed Head

The night lay heavy on the stone, And Xbalanque remained alone, Beside his brother, overthrown, Whose severed head the kings had shown Before the courts of Xibalba. No tears he shed, no cry he gave, Though death had opened wide the grave, For still he knew that thought might save A soul from dark Xibalba.

He called the creatures of the wood, Who came before him where he stood, And bade them offer what they could, That he might turn their gifts to good Against the kings of Xibalba. The forest stirred with foot and wing, And each came forth with what to bring, Yet none had brought the needful thing To conquer death in Xibalba.

A coati came, small and brown, Who rolled a squash before him down, As round and smooth as any crown That ever graced a king's renown Within the halls of Xibalba. And Xbalanque beheld its face, And lifted it with solemn grace, And set it in his brother's place Beneath the eyes of Xibalba.

Then from the heavens, far and high, The Heart of Sky drew softly nigh, And lent the fruit a living eye, And breathed where mortal lips were dry Below the courts of Xibalba. The features formed beneath his hand, The breath returned at his command, And Hunahpu arose to stand Among the dead of Xibalba.

But dawn approached the eastern height, And still the face was not quite right, When Heaven veiled the coming light, And soot was cast across the white And distant roofs of Xibalba. Thus Hunahpu could stand and speak, Though squash formed brow and lip and cheek; His stolen head, a trophy bleak, Still crowned the court of Xibalba.

The brothers formed their plan by night, To win the head by craft, not might, And set a rabbit out of sight, Where vines concealed him from the light Beyond the courts of Xibalba. “When once the rubber ball draws near, Leap through the leaves and disappear; They’ll think it bounded far and clear Beyond the courts of Xibalba.”

At dawn the kings came forth to stare, And hung the true head in the air; Yet Hunahpu stood living there, A squash for head, with face and hair, Upon the court of Xibalba. The lords, deceived by shape and skin, Commanded that the game begin; They cast the rubber ball within The stony court of Xibalba.

Xbalanque met the falling ball, And drove it high beyond the wall; It bounced where vines and shadows fall, The rabbit bounded at the call Beyond the courts of Xibalba. They saw a bounding shape outside, The lords pursued his leaping stride, And chased him through the country wide, Far from the courts of Xibalba.

While all the rulers chased the hare, The twins climbed up the court’s high stair, And took the true head hanging there, Then set the squash in open air Above the courts of Xibalba. His living head resumed its place, Warm blood returned to brow and face; He stood restored by Heaven’s grace, Alive once more in Xibalba.

The twins retrieved the ball and cried, “Come back! The ball was here outside!” The kings returned in wounded pride, And all resumed the game inside The ancient courts of Xibalba. Then, by the twins’ deceptive play, The squash replaced the ball that day, And Xbalanque struck it away Before the kings of Xibalba.

The borrowed head flew high and low, And passed for rubber, blow by blow, Until a last and mighty throw Split rind and flesh in one bright show Across the courts of Xibalba. Its yellow meat was torn apart, The seeds flew wide from rind and heart; Each lord beheld the greater art That shamed the kings of Xibalba.

The kings beheld the broken rind, And knew their eyes had been made blind, By cunning of a greater kind Than all the arts their courts designed To rule the souls of Xibalba. No word was spoken from the throne, No boast of victory was known, And every lord stood still as stone The wounded pride of Xibalba.

Yet One Death watched with burning hate, And Seven Death beside him sate, And both resolved to seal the fate Of those who mocked their ancient state Before the thrones of Xibalba. They whispered where the torches burned, And none who heard their counsel turned, For dreadful was the doom discerned Within the halls of Xibalba.

A furnace stood beneath the ground, Where walls of stone were built around, And fire consumed without a sound Whatever living flesh was bound To enter flames in Xibalba. There neither craft nor shield might save, Nor wit withstand the burning grave, And death at last would have the brave Who challenged him in Xibalba.

But Hunahpu had seen the flame, And Xbalanque had guessed the game, And both perceived the kings would claim By fire what none could win through shame Within the courts of Xibalba. They sought two seers, both old and wise, Who read the stars with clouded eyes, And knew what forms might yet arise Beyond the death of Xibalba.

To Xulu first the brothers spoke, And Pacam heard the words they woke, Of bodies burned and bones once broke, Of ashes scattered like the smoke Above the streams of Xibalba. “When all our mortal flesh is slain, And only shattered bones remain, Then grind them fine as falling rain, And cast them forth in Xibalba.”

“Let not the kings erect a stone, Nor bury us where none have grown; Let all our dust be downward thrown Where dark and restless waters moan Beneath the earth of Xibalba. For what the river takes away, It bears beyond the reach of clay, And death shall learn another day The secret kept from Xibalba.”

Then Xbalanque beheld his twin, And spoke of all that they might win, If both should dare the fire within, And let the final game begin Before the kings of Xibalba. No fear was in his brother's face, No longing for the upper place, For both had come to end the race Of tyrants ruling Xibalba.

The furnace roared beneath the floor, The kings assembled there once more, And bade the twins approach the door, As none had dared approach before The burning heart of Xibalba. And Hunahpu, with steady eye, Beheld the fire mounting high, Then heard his brother's soft reply: “We die to conquer Xibalba.”

End of Book 4
